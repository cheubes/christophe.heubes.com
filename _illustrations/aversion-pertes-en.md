## Definition

**Loss aversion** is a central principle of prospect theory, formulated by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky in 1979. It states that the psychological suffering caused by a loss is approximately **twice as intense** as the pleasure produced by an equivalent gain.

This is not a matter of objective value. It is a fundamental asymmetry of human psychology: losing $100 weighs psychologically as much as gaining $200.

> We don't play to win. We play not to lose.

## Why it matters

Loss aversion explains a multitude of irrational behaviors:

**In investing**: investors hold losing positions for too long (hoping to "recover") and sell winning positions too early (afraid to "lose again"). This is the disposition effect, described by Shefrin and Statman (1985).

**In management**: fear of losing existing benefits (salary, status, perks) is a far more powerful driver of resistance to change than the promise of future gain.

**In negotiation**: framing an offer as "avoiding a loss" rather than an "opportunity for gain" often makes it more persuasive.

**In public policy**: removing an existing benefit, even an inefficient one, sparks far more opposition than declining to create an equivalent one. Those who receive it experience its removal as a loss, whereas a benefit that never existed is only a missed gain.

## Examples

**The coin toss bet**: in *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011), Kahneman describes a simple bet: heads, you lose $100; tails, you win $150. Although the bet is favorable on average, most people turn it down. They typically need to be promised around $200, twice the loss, before they accept.

**The endowment effect**: we value an object we own more than the same object we don't have. In the experiment by Kahneman, Knetsch and Thaler (1990), participants who had been given a mug asked for about twice as much to part with it as the others were willing to pay to acquire it.

**Status quo bias**: we prefer to change nothing even when changing would be objectively advantageous, because any change involves potential losses.

**Marketing "losses"**: "don't miss this offer" or "last day to take advantage" directly exploit loss aversion to accelerate purchasing decisions.

## Countermeasures

**Choose actively**: don't let the status quo pass for the absence of a decision. Changing nothing is a choice too, with its own costs: spelling them out lets you compare it with the alternatives on equal terms.

**Count missed gains**: evaluate missed opportunities with the same rigor as actual losses.

**Decide in advance**: create pre-established rules for investment or divestment decisions.
