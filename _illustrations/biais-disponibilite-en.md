## Definition

The **availability bias** (or availability heuristic) is a mental shortcut: to assess the frequency or probability of an event, we rely on how easily examples come to mind. What is vivid, recent or emotionally charged seems more probable, even if it is statistically rare.

Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman described this heuristic in 1973. Its effects on risk perception were measured shortly afterwards by Sarah Lichtenstein, Paul Slovic and their colleagues (1978): people overestimate spectacular causes of death (tornadoes, homicides, botulism) and underestimate mundane but frequent ones (diabetes, stroke, heart disease).

> What is memorable is not what is probable.

## Why it matters

This bias distorts our assessment of risk and shapes both individual and collective decisions:

**Risk assessment**: intense media coverage of rare events (terrorist attacks, plane crashes) makes them more present in our minds, and therefore more feared, than far more frequent but silent dangers.

**Public policy**: governments overreact to spectacular events, while slow and invisible threats (obesity, tobacco, climate change) are chronically undertreated.

**Financial markets**: after a recent negative event, investors overestimate the probability that it will happen again.

## Examples

**The letter K**: Tversky and Kahneman (1973) asked whether, in English, the letter K appears more often in the first or the third position of words. A majority answered the first position, because words starting with K come to mind more easily. In reality, words with K in the third position are about twice as common.

**Terrorism and air travel**: after 9/11, many Americans chose to drive rather than fly. Gerd Gigerenzer (2004) estimated that around 1,500 additional people died on the roads in the following year, about six times the number of people killed aboard the four hijacked planes.

**Disaster insurance**: disaster insurance sales surge after an earthquake, then fall back as the memory fades.

**Project management**: managers who recently experienced a delayed project overestimate the risk of delay in their next estimates.

## Countermeasures

**Look up the base rates**: before estimating a risk, look up how often it actually happens rather than relying on the examples that come to mind.

**Be wary of the news**: ask yourself whether an event seems likely because it is frequent or because you have heard a lot about it.

**Keep a log**: record facts as they happen (incidents, delays, results) to judge on all cases rather than on the most recent or striking ones.
