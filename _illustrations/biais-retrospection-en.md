## Definition

**Hindsight bias** is the tendency, once an event has occurred, to believe you could have predicted it. Memory does not faithfully preserve our past states of uncertainty: it rewrites them in light of what we now know.

Baruch Fischhoff demonstrated this experimentally in 1975: participants told the outcome of a historical event judged it more likely than those who did not know it, and believed they would have predicted it themselves. He called it "creeping determinism": once the outcome is known, it seems to have been inevitable.

> Memory rewrites itself in light of the known outcome.

## Why it matters

This bias is particularly dangerous when evaluating past decisions:

**Decision evaluation**: post-mortems become inaccurate, because decisions are judged with the certainty of hindsight, without reproducing the uncertainty of the moment.

**Overconfidence**: if we feel we "knew" in advance, we mistakenly believe we are good forecasters.

**Learning culture**: in organizations, it leads to biased retrospectives ("it was obvious that project would fail") that look for culprits instead of understanding what was actually predictable.

## Examples

**Nixon's trips**: before Richard Nixon's 1972 trips to China and the USSR, Fischhoff and Beyth (1975) asked students to estimate the probability of various outcomes. Afterwards, these students remembered having given higher probabilities to the events that actually happened.

**Judging negligence**: Kamin and Rachlinski (1995) presented the case of a city deciding whether to take precautions against a flood. About a quarter of participants judged the precautions necessary. Among those told that the flood had occurred, more than half found the city negligent.

**Stock market crash**: "Everyone could see it was going to collapse." A retrospective certainty that erases the genuine uncertainty of the time.

**Election result**: "With a candidate like that, the result was never in doubt." Yet nobody announced it with confidence before the vote.

**Production bug**: "We should have tested that case anyway." But the case was not obvious before the bug occurred.

**Industrial accident**: after the fact, investigators underestimate how ambiguous the warning signals were before the event.

## Countermeasures

**Record your forecasts**: write down your estimates and reasoning before knowing the outcome, so you can compare them honestly afterwards.

**Judge the decision, not the outcome**: evaluate a decision on the information available when it was made, not on what happened next.

**Imagine the other outcomes**: before concluding that a result was predictable, ask yourself how you would have explained the opposite outcome.
